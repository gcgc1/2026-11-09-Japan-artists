# Six Japanese Artists Glossary

The working vocabulary for this course. Definitions are the ones the lessons use; the lessons never drift to a synonym when a term is defined here.

## Art

**Superflat**:
Takashi Murakami’s theory that Japanese visual culture — from Edo-period painting to manga and anime — shares a flattened, two-dimensional picture space; also the title of his 2000 catalogue essay and the 2001 MOCA exhibition he curated.
_Avoid_: “Japanese pop art” (too broad), “flat design” (a graphic-design term).

**Nihonga**:
Japanese-style painting using mineral pigments and traditional conventions; Murakami completed a doctoral programme in Nihonga at Tokyo University of the Arts.
_Avoid_: “traditional painting” (too vague).

**Infinity Nets**:
Yayoi Kusama’s paintings of thousands of small painted loops, begun in New York in 1958–59, which cover the canvas in a single all-over field.
_Avoid_: “polka dots” (the dots are a motif, not the series).

**Self-obliteration**:
Kusama’s term for the dissolution of the boundary between the self and the pattern, the room, or the crowd; repetition is the technology that produces it.
_Avoid_: “meditation”, “therapy” (they explain the feeling, not the method).

**Obliteration Room**:
A white domestic room that visitors gradually cover with coloured dot stickers; Kusama has installed versions since 2002.
_Avoid_: “dot room” (the work is about the visitors’ accumulation, not the artist’s).

**Narcissus Garden**:
Kusama’s 1966 installation of 1,500 mirrored spheres, first installed unofficially at the Venice Biennale and now remade in various places.
_Avoid_: “mirror balls” (the title matters).

**Aomori-ken (Aomori Dog)**:
Yoshitomo Nara’s 2005 reinforced-concrete dog, 8.5 m tall, permanently installed outdoors at the Aomori Museum of Art.
_Avoid_: “the big dog” (use the title).

**Kawaii**:
Cute, in Japanese; in Nara’s work, cuteness is a mask that makes menace and vulnerability visible at the same time.
_Avoid_: “kitsch” (kitsch is a judgement; kawaii is a cultural register).

**Otaku**:
Japanese fan culture organised around anime, manga, and collectibles; a central reference for Murakami’s Superflat.
_Avoid_: “nerd” (too loose).

**1981 Paris debuts**:
The first Paris shows by Rei Kawakubo and Yohji Yamamoto, in the same season, which made “Japanese fashion” a global category.
_Avoid_: “Japanese invasion” without quotation marks (it was the press’s phrase, not a neutral description).

## Fashion

**A-POC**:
“A Piece of Cloth” (1998–99): Issey Miyake’s system for knitting a tube of cloth on a computer-programmed machine, which the wearer cuts into garments.
_Avoid_: “seamless clothing” (it is a production system, not a finish).

**Pleats Please**:
Miyake’s 1993 line of garment-pleated polyester: the garment is cut and sewn oversized, sandwiched in paper, and heat-pressed into permanent pleats.
_Avoid_: “pleated fabric” (the pleating happens after construction).

**Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body**:
Rei Kawakubo’s spring/summer 1997 collection, also called “Lumps and Bumps”, in which padding distorts the body’s silhouette.
_Avoid_: “the lumpy collection” (use the title, then the nickname).

**In-between**:
The Met’s 2017 framing of Kawakubo’s work as occupying the space between categories — fashion/antifashion, object/subject, design/not design.
_Avoid_: “androgynous” (one possible effect, not the argument).

**Y’s**:
Yohji Yamamoto’s first label, founded in 1972; the name is pronounced “wise”.
_Avoid_: “Yohji line” (Y’s is a specific label).

**Y-3**:
Yamamoto’s collaboration with Adidas, founded in 2002; the name refers to the three stripes and Yamamoto.
_Avoid_: “Yohji sneakers” (the label is Y-3).

**Garment pleating**:
The process of pleating a finished garment rather than a flat length of fabric; the method that made Pleats Please possible.
_Avoid_: “fabric pleating” (that is the older method).

## Context

**Post-war generation**:
In this course, the artists born 1929–43 — Kusama, Miyake, Kawakubo, Yamamoto — whose childhoods were shaped by the war and the occupation.
_Avoid_: “the war generation” (too broad; Nara and Murakami are post-war too, but not of this cohort).

**Bubble economy**:
Japan’s asset-price bubble of the late 1980s, whose collapse shaped the 1990s art market and the “Lost Decade” narrative.
_Avoid_: “the Japanese miracle” (that refers to the earlier high-growth period).
