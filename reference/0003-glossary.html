Reference 03 / pocket vocabulary

Glossary

The words the course uses, and the words it refuses to use.

If you can use five of these correctly in front of a work, you can hold your own in the room. The definitions are deliberately opinionated; the “avoid” note is the synonym trap.

Art

Superflat
Takashi Murakami’s theory that Japanese visual culture — from Edo-period painting to manga and anime — shares a flattened, two-dimensional picture space; also the title of his 2000 catalogue essay and the 2001 MOCA exhibition he curated. Avoid: “Japanese pop art” (too broad), “flat design” (a graphic-design term).
Nihonga 日本画
Japanese-style painting using mineral pigments and traditional conventions; Murakami completed a doctoral programme in Nihonga at Tokyo University of the Arts. Avoid: “traditional painting” (too vague).
Infinity Nets
Yayoi Kusama’s paintings of thousands of small painted loops, begun in New York in 1958–59, which cover the canvas in a single all-over field. Avoid: “polka dots” (the dots are a motif, not the series).
Self-obliteration
Kusama’s term for the dissolution of the boundary between the self and the pattern, the room, or the crowd; repetition is the technology that produces it. Avoid: “meditation”, “therapy” (they explain the feeling, not the method).
Obliteration Room
A white domestic room that visitors gradually cover with coloured dot stickers; Kusama has installed versions since 2002. Avoid: “dot room” (the work is about the visitors’ accumulation, not the artist’s).
Narcissus Garden
Kusama’s 1966 installation of 1,500 mirrored spheres, first installed unofficially at the Venice Biennale and now remade in various places. Avoid: “mirror balls” (the title matters).
Aomori-ken (Aomori Dog) あおもり犬
Yoshitomo Nara’s 2005 reinforced-concrete dog, 8.5 m tall, permanently installed outdoors at the Aomori Museum of Art. Avoid: “the big dog” (use the title).
Kawaii かわいい
Cute, in Japanese; in Nara’s work, cuteness is a mask that makes menace and vulnerability visible at the same time. Avoid: “kitsch” (kitsch is a judgement; kawaii is a cultural register).
Otaku オタク
Japanese fan culture organised around anime, manga, and collectibles; a central reference for Murakami’s Superflat. Avoid: “nerd” (too loose).
1981 Paris debuts
The first Paris shows by Rei Kawakubo and Yohji Yamamoto, in the same season, which made “Japanese fashion” a global category. Avoid: “Japanese invasion” without quotation marks (it was the press’s phrase, not a neutral description).

Fashion

A-POC A Piece of Cloth
“A Piece of Cloth” (1998–99): Issey Miyake’s system for knitting a tube of cloth on a computer-programmed machine, which the wearer cuts into garments. Avoid: “seamless clothing” (it is a production system, not a finish).
Pleats Please
Miyake’s 1993 line of garment-pleated polyester: the garment is cut and sewn oversized, sandwiched in paper, and heat-pressed into permanent pleats. Avoid: “pleated fabric” (the pleating happens after construction).
Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body
Rei Kawakubo’s spring/summer 1997 collection, also called “Lumps and Bumps”, in which padding distorts the body’s silhouette. Avoid: “the lumpy collection” (use the title, then the nickname).
In-between
The Met’s 2017 framing of Kawakubo’s work as occupying the space between categories — fashion/antifashion, object/subject, design/not design. Avoid: “androgynous” (one possible effect, not the argument).
Y’s
Yohji Yamamoto’s first label, founded in 1972; the name is pronounced “wise”. Avoid: “Yohji line” (Y’s is a specific label).
Y-3
Yamamoto’s collaboration with Adidas, founded in 2002; the name refers to the three stripes and Yamamoto. Avoid: “Yohji sneakers” (the label is Y-3).
Garment pleating
The process of pleating a finished garment rather than a flat length of fabric; the method that made Pleats Please possible. Avoid: “fabric pleating” (that is the older method).

Context

Post-war generation
In this course, the artists born 1929–43 — Kusama, Miyake, Kawakubo, Yamamoto — whose childhoods were shaped by the war and the occupation. Avoid: “the war generation” (too broad; Nara and Murakami are post-war too, but not of this cohort).
Bubble economy
Japan’s asset-price bubble of the late 1980s, whose collapse shaped the 1990s art market and the “Lost Decade” narrative. Avoid: “the Japanese miracle” (that refers to the earlier high-growth period).
Ask your teacher If a term feels slippery, ask for a concrete example in a work. The glossary should be a tool, not a quiz. I can add a term here the moment you can use it correctly.
