How to look

Five questions for a work of art. Five for a garment. One minute of your own attention before the wall text.

Museums and stores are designed to tell you what to feel. The trick is to have a reaction first, then let the label complicate it. These prompts are deliberately simple so you can use them while standing up.

For an art work

01Scale

Is it bigger or smaller than your body? Does it fill the room, or ask you to lean in? Scale is the first thing the photograph loses.

Try it: at Naoshima, stand at the edge of the pier and let the pumpkin occupy your whole field of view. Then step back until it becomes a character in the landscape.

02Surface

Is the surface smooth, crusty, printed, mirrored, or hand-made? Does it look machine-perfect or visibly repetitive? The surface tells you how the thing was made.

Try it: in a Murakami flower painting, find one flower and follow its outline. Then find a place where the pattern breaks or a smiley face appears. The break is the joke.

03Repetition

What repeats — a mark, a motif, a body, a logo? Repetition can be a meditation, a market strategy, or a symptom. Ask which one this work wants.

Try it: in a Kusama Infinity Net, count ten loops, then stop counting and notice your eye trying to count anyway. That failure is the work.

04The edge

What happens at the boundary — of the canvas, the room, the plinth, the body? Kusama’s rooms dissolve the edge; Nara’s paintings often leave the figure floating in an empty field.

Try it: in an Infinity Mirror Room, find the corner where two mirrors meet. The seam is usually the only clue to the room’s size.

05Your body

Does the work ask you to walk, sit, queue, crouch, or look up? Installation art is completed by a body in space; a painting is completed by a body in front of it.

Try it: at the Aomori Dog, walk around it once before you take a photograph. The dog’s expression changes with the angle — or your mood does.

For a garment

01Silhouette

Trace the outline with your eye before you look at the details. Is it close to the body, away from it, or a shape of its own? Kawakubo’s 1997 padding makes a silhouette that is not a body.

Try it: in a Yamamoto coat, find the widest point. It is rarely the shoulder or the waist; it is often the space between the body and the cloth.

02Fabric

Does it hold a shape or collapse? Is it shiny or matte, stiff or fluid? Miyake’s pleats are a fabric technology before they are a look.

Try it: in a Pleats Please garment, pinch a pleat and let it go. The memory of the fibre is the design.

03Construction

Where are the seams? Is the garment cut from one piece, or many? Is the hem finished or raw? Construction is where the designer’s argument is most honest.

Try it: in a CDG piece, find a place where the expected seam is missing or moved. Asymmetry is not decoration; it is a decision about the body.

04Movement

What happens when the wearer walks? Does the cloth swing, drape, collapse, or stay still? A garment is a performance that only exists when worn.

Try it: watch a runway clip of Miyake or Yamamoto with the sound off. Follow one garment from the first step to the turn. The turn is the design.

05Styling

What is it worn with? What is left out? A garment on a mannequin is a proposition; a garment on a person is an argument. The styling tells you which one the designer wants.

Try it: in a store, take a piece off the rail and hold it against your body in a mirror. The “look” disappears; the cut remains.

The 60-second exercise

  1. Look for 20 seconds without reading anything. No phone, no label.
  2. Name three nouns you can see. (Dot, mirror, pumpkin.)
  3. Name one verb the work is doing. (Multiplying, swallowing, repeating.)
  4. Name one feeling in your body. (Dizzy, calm, watched, amused.)
  5. Now read the label. What did it add? What did it miss?
