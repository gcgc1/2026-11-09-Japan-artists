Lesson 04 / 17 min / Fashion

Black as a Beginning

Yohji Yamamoto (b. 1943) · 山本耀司

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why Yamamoto’s black is not a colour choice, and how his 1981 Paris debut changed the way clothes sit on the body.
Tokyo, b. 1943 Paris, 1981 Y’s Y-3

The three-minute version

Yohji Yamamoto was born in Tokyo in 1943. His mother was a dressmaker; he grew up in the aftermath of the war, in a city that was rebuilding itself. He studied law at Keio University, then changed direction and went to Bunka Fashion College. In 1972 he founded Y’s, his first label. The name is pronounced “wise”.

He debuted in Paris in 1981, in the same season as Rei Kawakubo. The clothes were black, oversized, asymmetric, and often looked unfinished. The French press was hostile; the images circulated anyway. Within a few years, the “Japanese designer” silhouette — black, draped, layered, gender-neutral — had become one of the defining looks of the decade.

In 1989 Wim Wenders made Notebook on Cities and Clothes, a film essay shot in Tokyo and Paris. It remains the best portrait of Yamamoto’s process: the fittings, the fabric, the doubts, the city. In 2002 he launched Y-3 with Adidas, taking the same silhouette into sportswear; in 2011 he published My Dear Bomb, a book of autobiographical fragments. He has also designed costumes for film and theatre, including Takeshi Kitano’s Dolls (2002).

Dates and context: Wim Wenders Stiftung; The Met, “Miyake, Kawakubo, and Yamamoto”; Adidas, Y-3.

Black is not minimalism

Yamamoto’s black is often mistaken for minimalism: the absence of colour, the rejection of ornament. But minimalism wants to reduce; Yamamoto wants to enlarge. Black is the condition that lets you see the cut, the weight, the way a sleeve falls, the difference between one matte wool and another. It is not there to make the wearer disappear. It is there to make the garment visible.

The silhouette is the argument. A Yamamoto coat often has more space between the body and the cloth than a conventional coat; the shoulders may drop, the hem may trail, the fastening may be off-centre. The body is not corrected or displayed. It is clothed, and given room.

The body is not corrected; it is clothed — and given room. A working summary of the Yamamoto silhouette, after the Met’s essay on Japanese fashion

This is why his clothes look so different on a moving person and so strange on a hanger. The garment needs a body to complete it, but it does not flatter the body in the usual sense. It proposes a different relationship: shelter, weight, asymmetry, a little melancholy.

Three works to know

1. The 1981 Paris debut

Black, oversized, layered, asymmetric. The press saw “bag lady” clothes; the clothes are now in museum collections. The debut matters less for any single garment than for the permission it gave: a designer could start from the cloth and the body’s movement, not from the hourglass. Kawakubo was doing something adjacent in the same season; together they made “Japanese fashion” a category, whether they wanted it or not.

2. Notebook on Cities and Clothes (1989)

Wenders’s film is not a conventional documentary. It is a diary, an essay, and a conversation between two people who work slowly with images. The camera follows Yamamoto through fittings and studio work; Wenders’s voiceover worries about whether film can ever capture clothing. It is the best primary source on Yamamoto, and the best answer to “why does this matter?”

3. Y-3 (2002–)

The collaboration with Adidas took the Yamamoto silhouette into sportswear: black, layered, technical, and unexpectedly wearable. It is easy to dismiss as commercial, but it is also a test of the argument. If the silhouette only works in a couture show, is it a real idea about clothes? Y-3 says yes — the same shape survives a sneaker.

Look closely

  • Find the widest point. It is rarely the shoulder or the waist. It is often the space between the body and the cloth.
  • Look for the asymmetry. A hem, a lapel, a fastening. The imbalance is a decision, not a mistake.
  • Watch the walk. A Yamamoto garment is designed for motion; the still image is a poor test.

Trip card · if you go

Where to see Yamamoto in person

  • Y’s / Yohji Yamamoto stores, Tokyo — the Aoyama and Ginza areas; the clothes are best seen in the hand, not behind glass.
  • Y-3 stores — the sportswear line; a different register of the same silhouette.
  • V&A, London — the fashion collection holds Yamamoto; check the galleries and study rooms.
  • The Met, New York — Costume Institute holdings; the 1980s and 1990s pieces are the deep end.
  • Palais Galliera, Paris — fashion museum with a strong Japanese-designer presence; check the schedule.

Yamamoto’s clothes are often shown on mannequins that underplay the volume. Ask for a runway image or a video if the museum has one; the garment is a performance.

Primary source

Watch Notebook on Cities and Clothes (1989) in full if you can. Then read My Dear Bomb (2011), Yamamoto’s own book of fragments. The film and the book together are worth more than any survey.

Ask your teacher If a garment looks “shapeless”, ask me to describe the pattern pieces. Yamamoto’s volumes are engineered; the shape is often a few very large, very precisely placed panels.

One thing to remember: black is not a colour here; it is a way of making the body disappear into the cloth.
