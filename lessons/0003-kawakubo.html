Lesson 03 / 17 min / Fashion

In-Between

Rei Kawakubo (b. 1942) · 川久保玲

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why Kawakubo’s clothes are not “weird fashion” but a set of arguments about the body, the category, and the store — and what “in-between” means in the 2017 Met exhibition.
Tokyo, 1969 Paris, 1981 Body Meets Dress Dover Street Market

The three-minute version

Rei Kawakubo was born in Tokyo on 11 October 1942. She studied fine arts, literature, and the history of aesthetics at Keio University, worked in advertising, and founded Comme des Garçons in 1969. She showed in Tokyo from 1975 and made her Paris debut in April 1981 — the same season as Yohji Yamamoto, and the moment the French press discovered “Japanese fashion”.

The early Paris clothes were black, oversized, asymmetric, and apparently unfinished. They hid the body instead of displaying it. The press was scandalised; the clothes are now in the permanent collections of the Met, the V&A, and the NGV. In 1997 she showed Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body, the collection everyone calls “Lumps and Bumps”: padding on the hips, back, and shoulders distorted the model’s silhouette, while gingham made the whole thing look domestic.

In 2004 she opened the first Dover Street Market in London, a store conceived as a changing installation. In 2017 the Met’s Costume Institute devoted a full retrospective to her, Art of the In-Between, with about 140 examples of womenswear from the early 1980s onward. She was still working in 2026, showing an installation of recent designs at the Independent art fair in New York.

Dates and framing: NGV, “Rei Kawakubo: reframing fashion”; The Met, 2017 exhibition.

The 1981 shock

Kawakubo and Yamamoto did not arrive in Paris as a coordinated movement. They arrived in the same season, with different labels and different arguments, and the press made them into a category. The clothes were black. They were big. They did not cinch the waist or lift the bust. They looked, to the French eye, like a refusal of the body.

That refusal is the beginning of the lesson. Kawakubo is not interested in making a woman look thinner, taller, or more desirable. She is interested in what happens when the garment stops obeying the body’s outline and starts making its own. Sometimes that looks elegant. Sometimes it looks like a tumour. The point is that the body is no longer the standard.

“Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body … really challenged the way the silhouette was constructed.” Francesca Granata, on MoMA’s audio guide

Three works to know

1. The 1981 Paris debut

Not a single garment but a collection: black, oversized, layered, asymmetrical, often with raw edges. It looks mild now because the language it invented has been absorbed into every minimalist wardrobe. In 1981 it read as an insult to French tailoring — and as a new idea of what a woman could wear.

2. Body Meets Dress, Dress Meets Body (1997)

The “lumps and bumps” collection. Padded forms were placed on the abdomen, hips, back, and shoulders, creating a silhouette that was neither pregnant nor thin nor symmetrical. Gingham made it look like a child’s dress. The press called the pads tumours; the collection is now a set piece in every history of fashion. It is the clearest example of Kawakubo’s method: start with the body, then refuse its given shape.

3. Art of the In-Between (2017)

The Met exhibition framed her career as a series of boundaries that her clothes occupy: fashion/antifashion, design/not design, object/subject, clothes/not clothes. The title is the best one-word summary of her work. She is not “against” fashion; she works in the gap between fashion and everything else.

The store as a work

Dover Street Market opened in London in 2004; the Ginza store followed in 2008. It is a department store designed like an exhibition that keeps changing: guest designers, raw finishes, clothes hung like specimens. Kawakubo’s retail is not a separate business from her fashion; it is the same argument about display, category, and surprise.

When you visit, do not look for a “look”. Look at how the clothes are placed. The store is telling you what Kawakubo thinks a garment is: an object with its own life, not a tool for making the wearer prettier.

Trip card · if you go

Where to see Kawakubo in person

  • Dover Street Market Ginza — the store as installation; the most direct way to see the current CDG universe.
  • Comme des Garçons Aoyama / Marunouchi — the flagship and the department-store concessions; ask to see the current collection, not just the perfume.
  • The Met, New York — the 2017 exhibition is over, but the Costume Institute’s collection and the fashion galleries remain the deepest historical view.
  • NGV, Melbourne — the Collecting Comme holdings, a major Kawakubo collection with more than sixty-five examples.
  • V&A, London — fashion galleries and study rooms; check access to the archive.

The clothes are often displayed flat or on abstract mannequins. That is deliberate: Kawakubo wants you to see the construction, not a body posing.

Primary source

Start with the Met’s exhibition page, then listen to Francesca Granata on MoMA’s audio guide. The 2017 catalogue, Rei Kawakubo/Comme des Garçons: Art of the In-Between, is the best single book on the work.

Ask your teacher If a garment looks “unwearable”, ask me to walk you through the construction. There is usually a precise engineering decision under the shock — and once you see it, the shock becomes the point.

One thing to remember: Kawakubo does not dress the body; she proposes a body.
