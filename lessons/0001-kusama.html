Dots Against Infinity

Yayoi Kusama (1929–2026) · 草間彌生

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain why a room full of dots is not a pattern — and what Kusama meant by self-obliteration. That single idea will make her paintings, her mirror rooms, and the pumpkin on Naoshima click together.
Post-war Japan New York, 1958–73 Infinity Nets Mirror rooms

The three-minute version

Kusama was born in 1929 in Matsumoto, a seed-growing town in the mountains of Nagano. Her family was prosperous and unhappy. From childhood she had hallucinations — fields of dots, flowers that spoke, patterns that multiplied until they swallowed her. Drawing was not a hobby; it was a way to fix the visions on paper before they fixed her.

She trained in nihonga in Kyoto, found it suffocating, and wrote to Georgia O’Keeffe in 1955 asking how to make it in America. In November 1957 she flew to Seattle; six months later she was in New York. By 1959 she had her first New York solo at Brata Gallery, showing the paintings that became the Infinity Nets: thousands of small white loops on a dark ground, painted with a brush so small the gesture almost disappears.

The 1960s were a burst: soft sculptures covered in stuffed cloth phalluses, mirror rooms, naked happenings against the Vietnam War, and Narcissus Garden, 1,500 mirrored spheres installed unofficially at the 1966 Venice Biennale and sold off one by one until the authorities stopped her. Then New York lost interest. She returned to Japan in 1973 and, within four years, checked herself into a Tokyo psychiatric hospital. She lived there for the rest of her life, working in a studio across the street.

The revival began in the late 1980s and became a global phenomenon after 1993, when she represented Japan at the Venice Biennale — the same exhibition that had once refused her a place. The 1994 Pumpkin on Naoshima, the 2017 opening of the Yayoi Kusama Museum in Tokyo, and the Infinity Mirrors tour turned her into one of the most visited artists in the world. She died on 14 August 2026, aged 97.

Dates and framing: The Guardian obituary, M+ timeline, Tate introduction.

The dots are a technology

The easy reading of Kusama is “polka dots”. The useful reading is that the dot is a unit of repetition, and repetition is a machine for producing a specific experience: the loss of the boundary between yourself and the world. Kusama called it self-obliteration.

This is not a metaphor she added later. It is the thing she was doing from the beginning. The Infinity Nets are not “a painting of a net”; they are a performance of endless small marks that refuses a centre, a hierarchy, or a place for the eye to rest. The dots on the pumpkin do the same thing at a different scale. The room does it at the scale of your body.

“I fight pain, anxiety, and fear every day, and the only method I have found that relieved my illness is to keep creating art.” Yayoi Kusama, quoted in The Guardian

The Broad’s essay puts it more formally: the Infinity Nets are “performances in paint, obsessions in space, and literally other worlds made visible.” That is the sentence to hold on to. The paintings are not objects that happen to be covered in marks; they are records of an action that could have gone on forever.

Source: The Broad, “Yayoi Kusama”; The Guardian.

Three works to know

1. Infinity Nets (1958–)

The paintings that made her name in New York. Small loops, painted one by one, covering the whole canvas. From a distance they look almost blank; up close they are thick, organic, and slightly alarming. The art-historical comparison is to Pollock, Rothko, and Newman — but the point is not “abstract expressionism with a Japanese accent”. It is that the mark is a unit of time, and the painting is a way of surviving time.

2. Infinity Mirror Room—Phalli’s Field (1965)

A room lined with mirrors, filled with hundreds of stuffed, polka-dotted protrusions. The mirrors multiply the forms into an apparently infinite field. This is the ancestor of every “Infinity Mirror Room” you have queued for. The title matters: the field is made of phalli, and the work is about fear and obsession, not just pretty lights.

3. Pumpkin (1994)

A yellow, black-dotted pumpkin on a pier at Naoshima, with the Seto Inland Sea behind it. The pumpkin is a childhood motif — her family grew seeds; she has said she was enchanted by their “charming and winsome form”. At Naoshima the sculpture is both a character and a horizon point. You can walk around it; the dots stay the same; the sea changes.

Look closely

Next time you are in front of a Kusama, try three things:

  • Count ten marks, then stop. Notice your eye trying to continue. That refusal to resolve is the work.
  • Find the edge. In a mirror room, look for the seam where two mirrors meet. It is usually the only evidence of the room’s size.
  • Notice your body. Are you standing still, queuing, leaning in, or walking? Kusama’s installations are completed by a body in space.

Trip card · if you go

Where to see Kusama in person

  • Matsumoto City Museum of Art — Kusama: The place for my soul, 19 September – 6 December 2026, plus the permanent collection. The hometown show is the deepest dive.
  • Yayoi Kusama Museum, Tokyo — The Light Spreads Out, and Glittering Things Start Appearing All Around Me, 8 October 2026 – 31 January 2027. Timed entry; closed Monday–Wednesday.
  • Naoshima — the outdoor Pumpkin (1994) on the pier; free, and best at dusk.
  • M+, Hong Kong — strong Kusama holdings; pair with Nara and Murakami.
  • Stedelijk Museum, Amsterdam — major retrospective, 11 September 2026 – 17 January 2027.

The pumpkin is outdoors; the museum is indoors. Both are worth the detour. Check the venue sites before booking — the 2026 memorial exhibitions are still being announced.
