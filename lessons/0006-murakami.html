Lesson 06 / 17 min / Art

Superflat

Takashi Murakami (b. 1962) · 村上隆

By the end of this lesson you will be able to explain Superflat in one sentence, and why Murakami’s art and his merchandise are the same argument — not a betrayal of it.
Tokyo, b. 1962 Nihonga PhD Kaikai Kiki Louis Vuitton

The three-minute version

Takashi Murakami was born in Tokyo on 1 February 1962. He studied nihonga — Japanese-style painting — at Tokyo University of the Arts and completed a doctoral programme in 1993. The training matters: he can paint in a traditional idiom, and he can also explain why the traditional idiom and anime share a picture plane.

In the 1990s he became the most visible Japanese artist of his generation, with characters such as DOB and a practice that blurred painting, sculpture, animation, and merchandising. In 2000 he published the essay A Theory of Super Flat Japanese Art; in 2001 he curated the exhibition Superflat at MOCA’s Pacific Design Center. The same year he founded Kaikai Kiki, the company that runs his studio, manages other artists, and produces editions.

The 2000s were a run of high-profile collaborations and museum shows: Louis Vuitton (2003), Little Boy at Japan Society (2005), Versailles (2010), and The 500 Arhats — a 100-metre painting made with a large team, first shown in Doha and then at Mori Art Museum in 2015–16. In 2024 the Kyoto City Kyocera Museum mounted Mononoke Kyoto, a large survey that put the Superflat argument back in the city of traditional painting.

Dates and framing: Kaikai Kiki; MOCA, Superflat; Gagosian.

Superflat in one sentence

Superflat is the argument that Japanese visual culture — Edo-period painting, ukiyo-e prints, manga, anime, graphic design, and the otaku culture that consumes them — shares a flattened, two-dimensional picture space, and that this flatness is not a lack of depth but a different kind of depth: a surface you can enter.

Murakami traces the line from Hokusai to 1970s television animation. The claim is deliberately provocative: it puts the museum canon and the convenience-store comic on the same historical plane. It also gives him a theory of his own work — the flowers, the DOB character, the smiling skulls are all “superflat” because they are flat, repeatable, and emotionally ambiguous.

“Superflatness … can create an escape from the pressures and expectations of everyday life.” Murakami’s argument, paraphrased by MOCA

That word “escape” is important. Murakami is not simply celebrating anime. He is describing a culture that uses cuteness and flatness as a way of managing pressure — the pressure of work, of school, of a crowded society. The flowers are cheerful. The cheerfulness is the symptom.

Three works to know

1. DOB (1993–)

DOB is a character that is also a self-portrait: a round, toothy head with the letters D-O-B, derived from a Japanese nonsense phrase. He appears in paintings, sculptures, animations, and merchandise. DOB is the clearest example of Murakami’s method: a cute mascot that can carry a museum painting, a toy, and a critique of the market at the same time.

2. My Lonesome Cowboy (1998)

A life-size nude anime figure with a lasso of semen — a deliberately outrageous fusion of otaku fantasy and American Western iconography. It sold for $15.2 million at Sotheby’s in 2008, then the record for a Murakami. The sculpture is a test case for the whole practice: is it a critique of the otaku economy, or a luxury product for the same economy? Murakami’s answer is usually “both”.

3. The 500 Arhats (2012)

A 3 × 100 metre painting, made with a team of art students and assistants, first shown in Doha and then at Mori Art Museum in 2015–16. The subject is Buddhist arhats, but the scale and the production line are the real subject: a premodern religious image made with a contemporary studio system. After the 2011 disaster, it is also Murakami’s attempt to make a painting that can hold grief.

The problem (which is also the point)

Murakami is the artist in this course who makes the art-market question impossible to avoid. He runs a company. He produces editions. He collaborates with Louis Vuitton. His studio has assistants. His characters appear on keychains. The critic’s instinct is to say that the merchandise contaminates the art. Murakami’s practice says the distinction was always false: the mascot, the painting, and the keychain are made by the same culture, for the same audience, with the same visual language.

That does not settle the argument. It makes it the subject. The best Murakami works are the ones where you can feel the tension between the handmade and the reproduced, the sacred and the cute, the critique and the sale. The 500 Arhats is on the handmade side; the keychain is on the other; the interesting work is in between.

Trip card · if you go

Where to see Murakami in person

  • Kaikai Kiki Gallery, Tokyo — the studio’s own gallery in Motoazabu; check the current show.
  • Mori Art Museum, Tokyo — the venue for The 500 Arhats (2015–16); still the best large-scale contemporary art stop in the city.
  • Kyoto City Kyocera Museum of Art — the 2024 Mononoke Kyoto survey; check the current programme.
  • MFA Boston — the museum has shown Murakami; check the current collection and exhibition.
  • M+, Hong Kong — the regional collection; pair with Nara and Kusama.

Primary source

Read the MOCA page for Superflat, then Murakami’s own essay in the 2000 catalogue. If you want the market argument, the ©Murakami catalogue (Brooklyn Museum, 2008) is the best place to see the museum and the merchandise in the same room.

Ask your teacher If a Murakami looks “just cute”, ask me to find the place where the image breaks — a skull, a tear, a fang, a weird hand. The break is where the argument starts.

One thing to remember: Murakami’s greatness and his problem are the same: he made the border between art and merchandise disappear.
